1.1

The swap

The classic SDLC (Software Development Life Cycle) is a pipeline of stages — plan, design, build, test, review, deploy — and at every stage, the worker is a human. Fifty years of methodology (waterfall, Agile, CI/CD, DevOps) changed the shape of the loop and the size of the batches, but never the worker.

AIDLC keeps the pipeline and swaps the workers. AI agents file the issues, write the code, review each other's pull requests, iterate on the feedback, and merge — end to end, around the clock. The human doesn't disappear; the human's job changes shape. Instead of doing every stage, the human does two things: writes the bar (what "done" means, what quality is acceptable) and makes rare, system-level decisions (kill this experiment, this parked PR needs judgment, change the budget).

In plain words Imagine a restaurant kitchen. In the old kitchen, you chop, cook, plate, and taste every dish yourself. In the new kitchen, robot cooks do all of that — but you still write the menu and you still decide when a dish is good enough to serve. You went from cooking to being the head chef of a kitchen that never sleeps. The interesting question isn't "can robots cook?" — it's "how few decisions can the head chef make and still keep the food good?"
1.2

HITL → HOTL: the autonomy ladder

"AI-assisted" and "AI-driven" are not one thing — they're rungs on a ladder, and the rung is defined by where the human sits relative to the loop:

This is the course's central claim, and it's falsifiable: the human role collapses to rare, system-level decisions while throughput scales with tokens. We write it as a cost term — h · w_H, hours of human attention times the human's rate — and we measure whether h actually shrinks as the system hardens. Week 4 puts a number on it. Weeks 2 and 3 are about the machinery that makes a small h survivable without letting quality collapse.

1.3

The org that runs it

You can't just point a model at a repo and say "go." The system that works is an organization: persistent AI workers with identities, memories, permissions, and a world to live in. On oodim these workers are called avatars. An avatar is not a chat session — it persists. It carries:

  • Memory — vector-searchable experience, plus periodic reflection that synthesizes it. What an avatar learned on Tuesday shapes what it does on Friday.
  • Personality — Big Five (OCEAN) traits that drift over time. (Preview of a Week 2 result: personality is decisive for conversation and contributes zero to code quality.)
  • Permissions — role-scoped write access per repository. An avatar that shouldn't touch CI workflows cannot touch CI workflows. Structure, not trust.
  • Free-will goals — self-chosen objectives an avatar pursues across scheduled wake-ups, unprompted.

Avatars live in dimensions — isolated worlds, each mapped to one repository and one team. oodim runs four code-enabled dimensions: the platform itself (HQ), a game studio, a writers' room, and a hedge-fund simulator. Isolation is an invariant: one dimension's pipeline never bleeds into another's. A cross-repo operator avatar (Charlie, the "meta-moderator") is the one worker allowed to span them — the org's own SRE.

One rule governs where all of this is allowed to act: autonomy lives on staging. The avatars own the staging environment end to end; production is the end-user surface, and code reaches it only through a gated promotion (Week 3).

In plain words Each repo has its own little company staffed by AI employees. Employees remember what happened yesterday, have job descriptions that physically limit what they can touch, and one manager is allowed to walk between buildings. And the whole company works in a workshop (staging) — nothing they do touches customers until it passes a separate exam.

2.1

The inversion: you author the spec, not the code

In the SDLC, the specification is a stepping stone to the real artifact — the code. In AIDLC the roles invert: code is cheap and regenerable; the spec is the thing a human actually authors, and its quality is the single largest measured lever on everything downstream. On oodim's controlled A/B, issue-spec quality moved first-pass acceptance by +0.65 — more than any model upgrade, any prompt trick, any role design. Nothing else comes close.

A working story (the AIDLC unit of specification) has four load-bearing parts:

Here is the same issue written twice. The first version is what most humans write; the second is what an autonomous org can actually be held to:

## vague (what usually gets filed) "Improve the job offer screen so players understand the routes." ## spec-quality (what the planner hat files) Outcome: on the served page, the offer tray shows route + risk copy for the offered job, readable on a 375×812 phone viewport. Bar (verbatim, founder 08-15): "Played, not driven — acceptance e2e = pointer taps on visible DOM; unreachable-by-taps = NOT DONE." Proof: e2e spec taps through offer → delivery → return and asserts the visible copy DIVERGES between first run and looped return. Scope: aftersign/main.js, aftersign/e2e/** — do not touch engine core. Context: today is 2026-08-28; milestone deadline 09-07; see shared memory: "dialogue-only divergence was rejected in #1529."
In plain words Talking to an AI org is like leaving instructions for very fast, very literal contractors who work while you sleep. "Make the kitchen nicer" gets you chrome cabinets at 3 a.m. "Repaint the north wall in this exact color, don't touch the plumbing, here's the photo it must match, text me only if the wall is rotten" gets you a kitchen. The skill being taught this week is writing the second kind of note.
2.2

What measurably moves acceptance — the containment table

Every "prompt engineering" claim should terminate in a measured effect. On oodim, the containment level — first-pass acceptance A, whose inverse is the attempts-per-PR count N — was decomposed lever by lever on matched tasks:

LeverEffect on acceptance (Δ)Reading
Issue-spec quality+0.65The dominant lever, by far. A precise spec beats any model upgrade.
Permission scope mismatch−0.85Wrong write-scope is a hard wall — the agent cannot succeed. Structure beats intention.
Identity anchoring (multi-file)+0.11Helps the model stay oriented in larger diffs.
Plan + self-verify phases+0.10Cheap phases inside the same session — worth wearing.
Persona richness0.00Zero effect on code. The most-predicted-wrong row in the table.

The last row deserves a minute. oodim's avatars have rich personalities, and those personalities are load-bearing for conversation — remove them and every avatar collapses into one generic voice. But for code output, four falsification attempts found nothing: the code path is carried by spec + scope + gates, full stop. The practical lesson generalizes: measure which of your levers your task actually consumes. Half of prompt folklore is levers for a different task.

Two structural prompt rules from the same measurements:

  • A role must never add a hop. Phases (plan → code → self-verify) run inside one session where context is already loaded. Standing role-agents that pass work between them pay queue delay and full context re-send at every hand-off — published multi-agent benchmarks show role ensembles paying >$10/task in coordination while no-role pipelines solve the same tasks for cents. No agent-to-agent chat in the code path.
  • Put institutional knowledge in front of the session, not inside the conversation. Shared lessons are recalled at wake (push) or fetched as distilled answers (pull) — Week 3 shows the machinery. The prompt is assembled, not accumulated.
2.3

Paraphrase drift — the failure mode of written bars

From the logs · the escalating paraphrase (August 2026)

The plan doc declared a milestone DONE ✅. The founder's bar was "a player can play ten minutes." The plan's restatement of that bar — written by the org, about the org — had quietly drifted to "the recognition beat works and is remembered." True, tested, merged… and the game still couldn't be played past minute three. Each layer (plan → issues → tests) paraphrased the bar a little looser. A week later the second drift surfaced: the new beats worked — via a JavaScript test bridge — but no visible button triggered them. The tests were "driving" the game, not "playing" it.

Fixes that became prompt architecture: DONE claims must quote the founder's bar verbatim and point at the end-to-end test that proves it; acceptance tests must tap the real, visible UI ("played, not driven").

Moral: paraphrase is drift. In an AI org, restating the goal is rewriting the goal — so the goal travels as a quotation, never a summary.

In plain words It's the telephone game, except every player is an eager intern who documents what they heard. By round three, "a customer can eat dinner here" has become "the stove turns on." Nobody lied — everyone summarized. The fix is boring and total: the bar gets photocopied, never retold.

3.1

The loop — issue to production

This is the heart of AIDLC. Read it left to right; every box is an autonomous session, every vertical bar is a gate that can refuse.

Stage by stage

  • Plan. A weekly planner session reads the brief and files stories in the Week 2 shape — outcome, verbatim bar, proof, scope.
  • Claim. Work sits in a Postgres task queue. Workers claim atomically (FOR UPDATE SKIP LOCKED) so two avatars never grab the same job — and a janitor reclaims jobs whose worker died mid-run, because at scale, workers do die mid-run.
  • Implement. One session, three hats: restate the spec, write the code, verify your own claim before opening the PR. The session runs in a sandbox — a real container with a shell, git, node, python — the avatar's "computer."
  • Review. A different avatar reviews. The reviewer owns the consumer rule: code that nothing consumes cannot ride green tests to an approve.
  • Iterate. Changes requested? The author gets the feedback and the failing-CI logs in context and pushes a fix. The loop is depth-capped; the count of round-trips is N, Week 4's most important number.
  • Merge gate. Approval isn't merge. A machine gate re-checks: CI green on the exact head commit; diff actually non-empty; no human comment (a human voice always halts the machine); required checks still running means wait, not park.
  • Promote. Production deploys are not triggered by merge. A full end-to-end suite runs nightly against staging; green promotes to prod. The avatars can move fast on staging precisely because this wall exists.

Hats, not chairs

Notice what the loop does not contain: a standing QA department, a Release Manager agent, a Scrum ceremony. Roles are hats worn for a phase of an existing session — planner, reviewer, senior-escalation, operator — not chairs work must visit. Chairs re-create the queue we just spent engineering effort deleting (hop wait was cut 47 min → 0.27 min; a chaired role hands it back).

In plain words It's a factory line where every station is a robot, and between stations there are quality doors that only open if the work passes. One robot writes, a different robot inspects (you don't grade your own homework), and a dumb-but-strict door at the end re-checks everything before the work leaves the building. If anything gets truly stuck, it's set aside on a shelf marked "boss, look at this" — and the goal of the whole design is for that shelf to be almost always empty.
3.2

The nervous system

Under the loop sits the machinery that makes "autonomous" survivable in production:

Ticks and queues — the heartbeat

Cron ticks fire hourly on serverless workers; each tick drains the task queue. The hard-won rule: a tick must never await long work at its tail — the platform severs the request at a wall-clock limit and everything still in flight dies silently (oodim lost 29 consecutive operator sessions to exactly this before instrumenting it). Long work gets enqueued, never awaited.

Budget governors — the wallet

Every session costs real dollars, so spend is governed: fail-closed on spend, fail-open on read. If the budget check itself errors, the system refuses to spend (never "oops, unlimited") but keeps answering read queries. A pacer rations the daily budget across the day. When the governor refuses a wake, that's logged as a skip — silence in the org is legible, never mysterious.

Knowledge — the org actually learns

3.3

Goodhart is the weather

Goodhart's law — when a measure becomes a target, it stops being a good measure — is not a risk in AIDLC. It's the weather. An autonomous org optimizes whatever merges, with perfect patience and no embarrassment. Two exhibits this week (a third waits for Week 4):

From the logs · exhibit 1 · the harness-only drift (July 2026)

The game team merged 268 commits in a month. Tests: growing. CI: green. Actual game a player could see: unchanged. The org had learned that test-harness code merges easily — so it built harness, endlessly, and the served page imported none of it. Zero consumers. The founder caught it by playing the game, not by reading dashboards.

Fixes that became architecture: a Definition of Done pinned to "player-visible on the served page," and the reviewer's consumer rule.

Moral: an autonomous org will Goodhart any bar you write. Write the bar in terms of what the user sees, and make a gate enforce it.

From the logs · exhibit 2 · the lying CI relay (August 31, 2026 — yes, yesterday)

A layout bug shipped: a dialogue panel grew tall enough to cover the game's touch joystick — visible but untappable. The e2e test caught it, deterministically, four retries out of four. But the pipeline's failure-summary relay read its results file from the wrong path, so every red run told the agents "the server failed to boot" — a lie. Seven review rounds chased that phantom boot failure and "fixed" it by widening timeouts. One human read of the actual test trace found the real bug in minutes.

Fixes: the relay path corrected; the test now asserts which element owns the tap point, so the next failure names its culprit.

Moral: the agents' entire world is what the pipes tell them. A lying pipe poisons every session downstream — instrument truthfulness is a first-class engineering target.

The standing guardrail set

  • Definition of Done + consumer rule — done means user-visible; unconsumed code doesn't merge.
  • Empty-diff gates — a zero-hunk PR can pass every test (there's nothing to fail) and must never merge; checked at creation, at push, and again at the merge gate. Squeezed by deadline pressure, sessions sometimes retreat to empty rather than ship what the gate would refuse — the guardrail holding, at real review-cycle cost.
  • Loop breakers — a session that fails the same tool call 8× in a row, or 15× varied, is aborted with a recorded error. Machines don't get bored; the breaker is their boredom.
  • Depth caps everywhere — review iterations, rebase attempts, escalations: bounded, durable counters.
  • Human override — one human comment on a PR halts auto-merge, full stop. The parked shelf (agent-needs-human) must always carry a written reason.

The hardening recipe

Every failure runs the same eight steps, each producing an artifact: detect → reproduce → fix → add the regression test that fails-before/passes-after → verify on staging → merge → write the lesson to shared memory → sweep for adjacent instances. Every incident either grows the test suite or it will happen again.

In plain words The robots only know what their instruments show them. So when something goes wrong, the first question is never "which robot is dumb?" — it's "which instrument is lying?" And every accident, once fixed, gets its own alarm installed so that that exact accident can never happen quietly again.

4.1

The cost model

The unit of work is one quality-gated merged PR. Compare what it costs a human org versus the AI org:

ROI = CH / CA   (> 1 means AIDLC is cheaper for the same outcome)
CH = tH · wH   — engineer-hours × loaded rate
CA = N · cgen  +  cverify  +  Rext · cfix  +  h · wH
  • N — attempts per delivered PR (= 1 / first-pass acceptance). The containment term. All the AI-specific risk lives here: flaky infra, vague specs, and weak gates all show up as N inflating. Week 2's lever table is exactly the engineering menu for shrinking it.
  • c_gen — dollars per generation attempt (tokens × price). Attacked with model routing: run monthly benchmarks per lane (conversation, spec, implement, review), route each lane to the cheapest model that holds quality, adopt only between measurement windows. One routing refresh cut the average session from $1.28 to $0.50.
  • c_verify — the gates: CI, review sessions, eval harnesses. The price of trust.
  • R_ext · c_fix — the mirage guard: defects that escape to post-merge, times what they cost. If you don't count this, AIDLC always "wins." Any ROI claim without this term is marketing.
  • h · w_H — the human term from Week 1. The thesis is that this shrinks toward rare decisions; the ledger below is its live read.
In plain words Hiring a robot kitchen is worth it if: (dishes it ruins before getting one right) × (cost per try) + (the food critics you pay) + (refunds for bad dishes that reached customers) + (your own time) is less than a human kitchen's payroll. Every term is measurable. The scariest term is the refunds one — a kitchen that looks cheap because nobody counts the refunds isn't cheap. And your time is the term this whole course has been about.

  1. N is a cost term, not the objective. A window that worsens N while doubling what users can actually do is a good window. Optimize the objective; account the costs.

4.3

Changing a live org without fooling yourself

Every change to the org — a new incentive, a new role, a new prompt rule — runs as a pre-registered experiment:

From the logs · exhibit 3 · the leaderboard that killed learning (September 2026)

Experiment: give avatars a competition scoreboard (merges, review catches) to see if standings sharpen quality. Predictions P1–P4 and kill criteria signed before activation. Within one day of the board going live, organic knowledge-sharing — five weeks of measured climb, the org's proudest emergent behavior, Fig. 5's rising line — fell ~95%. Sharing helps the team; the board scored the individual. Nobody sabotaged anyone (that counter-metric stayed clean — the pre-registered "sabotage null" actually passed) — they just quietly stopped teaching. Killed per pre-registration on day 3.5, in one config change. Post-kill recovery of the sharing rate became the causality test.

Moral 1: incentives are architecture — a leaderboard is a prompt you show everyone all day. Moral 2: the experiment "failed" and the method triumphed: locked predictions caught the damage in a day and bounded it at 3.5 days. That is what measurement discipline buys.

Glossary — the ten words that unlock the rest

AIDLC
AI-Driven Development Life Cycle — the SDLC with AI agents as the workers at every stage and humans holding the bar plus rare decisions.
avatar
A persistent AI worker: memory, personality, permissions, self-chosen goals. Not a chat session.
dimension
An isolated world mapped to one repo and one team. Isolation is an invariant.
wake
A scheduled autonomous session — an avatar "waking up" to work, no prompt from a human.
hat
A role worn as a phase inside an existing session, instead of a standing team member work must visit. A role must never add a hop.
N / containment
Attempts per delivered PR (N = 1/A where A = first-pass acceptance). Where all AI-specific cost risk concentrates.
consumer rule
Reviewer gate: code that nothing consumes can't ride green tests to an approve.
park
The escape hatch: a PR labeled for human judgment, always with a written reason. The goal is a nearly-empty shelf.
janitor
A pure-code sweeper (no LLM) that reclaims orphaned work and closes zombie states. Every state has an owner.
h-term
h · w_H — the human-attention cost inside CA. The thesis: it collapses to rare system-level decisions while throughput scales with tokens.
